\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}

When I came to UiO, Morten read my background and said: ``You know PINNs, you know
many-body quantum systems. I want you to do PINNs on quantum dots.'' I said
``sure,'' and then asked the only sensible follow-up question I had:
``Where do I start?''

This thesis is the long answer. The short one took me the better part of two
years to find, and it is this: you start by arranging to be wrong in a way you
can measure.

There is a particular satisfaction in variational methods, and that is exactly
what it consists of. The variational principle guarantees that any trial
wavefunction yields an energy above the true ground state. Progress is therefore
unambiguous---the energy goes down or it does not---and the direction of error is
fixed by a theorem. You are never in doubt about whether you have improved. What
remains open is only how close you can get, and at what cost. For a project with
as few fixed landmarks as this one had, that guarantee was often the only solid
ground available.

I say ``two years'' deliberately. I have been fortunate in my supervisor: Morten
gave me not the customary year but nearly two, and the difference is the whole
thesis. Most of what is worth reading here comes from the second year, and none
of it would have been found on a schedule that ended at the first. That is a form
of trust it is easy to underestimate until you have needed it.

It was, even so, a solitary way to spend them. Not for want of a literature---
neural quantum states are a busy field, and FermiNet, PauliNet, and neural
backflow were all there to learn from. But the particular thing I was trying to
do, training a many-body ground state by collocation with no Markov chain
anywhere in the optimisation, had very little in front of it to steer by. When an
experiment failed there was frequently no way to tell whether the method was
unsound, the architecture too weak, or the code quietly broken; and for long
stretches the honest answer was that I did not know. My friends at CCSE, and
outside it, are the reason those stretches remained survivable rather than
merely long.

What kept me at it was a question that turned out to be more interesting than
the one I was assigned. Nearly every established method for the many-body
Schr\"odinger equation is built outward from physical principles---an
approximation is chosen, justified physically, and then implemented. I wanted to
approach it from the other end: solve the problem by machine learning, largely on
machine-learning terms, and only then ask what the solution had understood. A
trained network is not merely an answer; it is an answer with an internal
structure that can be examined. Twenty electrons in two dimensions is forty
degrees of freedom, and a configuration-interaction treatment of that would
outlast the age of the universe. But the wavefunction that eventually solves it
need not be forty-dimensional in any meaningful sense. So: how many directions
does the trained state actually vary along? How does correlation change as
particles are added, or as the trap is opened and they are given room to move
apart? What is actually required to reach a solution---and how must a network
grow, if at all, to keep pace with the system it is solving?

Those questions have answers here, and they are more specific than I expected.
The correlator settles onto a manifold of one or two effective dimensions and
stays there across three decades of confinement; what changes is its content, not
its size. And the difference between architectures turns out not to be capacity
but \emph{alignment}: a message-passing network keeps its dominant directions
pointed along the collective modes the Hamiltonian actually has, while a
separable network reaches the very same energy with much of its capacity spent on
directions no compact physical description resolves. At strong confinement this
costs nothing and is invisible. Open the trap and it is the whole story.

Then the trained states can be turned around and used as instruments. Read from
the inside, they show six electrons forming a single ring poised between two
shell topologies, twelve settling into two commensurate shells, twenty building
three and collapsing back toward one within a single decade of confinement. That
was the part I had not anticipated when I said ``sure'': that the network would
stop being the object of study and become the apparatus.

Yet the moments I learned the most from were, without exception, the failures.
An error in the importance-sampling code went undetected for months, because its
signature---accuracy decaying at larger particle numbers and weaker
confinement---is precisely the signature of an architecture that is merely not good
enough. In $Nd$ dimensions an error in a log-density grows linearly with
dimension and is amplified catastrophically in the normalised weights; the bug was
invisible exactly where experiments were cheap and ruinous exactly where they
mattered. A Langevin refinement step, which I had every intuition was an
improvement, introduced a systematic gradient bias, because importance weights
are valid only for samples drawn from the proposal and not for samples carried
along a non-equilibrium path. A second-order optimiser degraded precisely when the
landscape became ill-conditioned enough to require it, because the estimate of the
metric it inverts collapses into noise at small effective sample size. Discovering
the first of these did real damage to my confidence in everything else, and
re-establishing that confidence---rather than assuming it---is a substantial part
of what the later chapters are actually doing.

In each case the failure specified something no success could have: exactly what
the method had been quietly assuming, and exactly where the problem quietly
violated it. Negative results of this kind are not a concession or an
embarrassment. They are a method, and I have written them up as one.

Something else surfaced along the way that I have not stopped thinking about.
The obstacles in this work have a persistent double character: each is
simultaneously a physics problem and an optimisation problem, and it is usually
arbitrary which name you give it. Stochastic reconfiguration, invented by
physicists to optimise variational wavefunctions, is natural gradient descent on
the variational manifold. The REINFORCE estimator from reinforcement learning is
the score-function gradient of the variational energy. The Fisher information
matrix of statistics is the quantum geometric tensor of physics. These were not
borrowed by one field from another; they were found independently, by communities
that rarely read each other's papers. Coming to recognise that they are the same
mathematics felt less like discovering a bridge between two fields than like
realising there had only ever been one, approached from opposite sides.

What I can claim at the end of it is narrower than what I hoped for at the
beginning, and I have tried to keep it that way. A compact, well-conditioned
ansatz, trained by a scheme that is stable across three decades of confinement,
matches diffusion Monte Carlo wherever a diffusion Monte Carlo reference exists.
Below that---which is to say, throughout the strongly correlated regime this
thesis cares most about---there is no external benchmark, and the energies are
variational predictions supported by internal consistency and physical scaling
rather than validated numbers. I have said so in every place it is true. The
temptation to phrase these results as a competition with the best available
methods was considerable, and resisting it is, I think, one of the more
defensible decisions in the thesis.

Which returns me to hindsight. Looking back over this work, my first instinct is
to catalogue what I would now do differently---and it is a long catalogue, and it
is very easy to assemble when you already possess the knowledge you spent two
years acquiring. But most of that catalogue was purchased by the failures, and it
could not have been read off in advance from anything. The regret and the result
are the same object seen from two sides. That is worth remembering, and easy to
forget, which is the best argument I know for writing a preface at all.

\bigskip

A word on how to read what follows. This thesis proceeds through three ordered
decisions that anyone applying neural networks to the many-body Schr\"odinger
equation has to make: what to parameterise, how to train, and how to optimise.
They are simple to state and genuinely difficult to make correctly. The record of
what happens when they are made incorrectly is, in the end, more instructive than
any table of final energies---which is, at last, the answer to the question I
asked Morten on the first day. You start by being willing to be wrong on the
record, and then you keep the record.
