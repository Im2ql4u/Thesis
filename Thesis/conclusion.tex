\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This thesis set out to answer a concrete question: what must a practitioner decide
when applying physics-informed neural networks to the quantum many-body Schrödinger
equation, and what do those decisions cost?
The answer, worked out on two-dimensional harmonic quantum dots, is organized into
three layers that correspond to the chronological order in which the decisions arise.
This section returns to each, states what was learned, and identifies what remains open.

\paragraph{Layer 1\,---\,The ansatz: physical constraints before optimization.}
The first decision---what wavefunction to parameterize---is prior to training and
shapes everything that follows.
Antisymmetry is non-negotiable: the Slater determinant enforces it exactly.
Permutation equivariance of the correlator is guaranteed by the Deep Sets
architecture of the particle branch, but particle-only pooling cannot represent
two-body correlations; the pairwise branch is not optional for correlated systems.
The Kato cusp must be encoded analytically: learning it wastes capacity and the
optimizer cannot be trusted to discover the correct singular behavior under
noisy gradient estimates.
Working in trap-unit coordinates---$\tilde{\mathbf{r}} = \sqrt{\omega}\,\mathbf{r}$---aligns
the input dynamic range across the full $(N,\omega)$ grid and keeps the soft-core
pair features and backflow gates consistent across three decades of confinement.

These are not hyperparameter choices.
They are the answer to the question ``what do the physical laws of the problem
demand from the parameterization?'', and the correct answer to that question must
be established before the first gradient step.
The consequence of getting it right is that a compact ansatz---fewer than
$50\text{k}$ parameters---reaches near-DMC accuracy for $N\in\{2,6,12\}$ across
the full $\omega$ grid.
The consequence of getting it wrong is that instabilities are misattributed to
the optimizer or the training paradigm, and no amount of tuning resolves them.

Representation analysis reveals a further structural fact: the Jastrow correlator
and the backflow displacement field live in qualitatively different function spaces.
The correlator operates on a low-dimensional manifold ($r_{\rm eff} \lesssim 3$)
whose leading axis reorganizes its content across regimes without expanding its
rank.
The backflow field has intrinsic dimension an order of magnitude larger, consistent
with the theoretical expectation that a field encoding all pairwise displacements
in $Nd$-dimensional space requires a richer representation.
This separation has a practical implication: at large $N$, the memory cost of
backflow ($\mathcal{O}(N^2 \times h_{\rm bf})$) competes directly with the batch
size needed for reliable gradient estimates, and the correct response is to allocate
that memory to oversampling rather than to network capacity.

\paragraph{Layer 2\,---\,The training paradigm: where neural QMC fits.}
The second decision is how to compute energies and train.
The hierarchy of established methods---HF, FCI, and DMC---provides the frame
against which this decision can be evaluated.
HF misses correlation; FCI is exact but exponentially expensive.

Neural VMC with MCMC sampling reaches near-DMC accuracy for the quantum dots
studied here, at a computational cost far below FCI, with the additional property
that it is differentiable
end-to-end and can be trained on any $(N,\omega)$ combination without precomputed
basis expansions.
The MCMC-free collocation pipeline is the methodological contribution: a training
procedure with no Markov chain at any stage, which achieves $+0.009\%$ relative
error at $(N{=}6,\,\omega{=}1.0)$ and sub-$0.25\%$ across all five confinement
strengths for $N{=}6$.
This directly challenges the assumption that accurate neural wavefunction
optimization requires equilibrated Markov chains.
The binding constraint is the effective sample size (ESS) of the
importance-weighted batch, which degrades as $e^{-cNd}$ as confinement weakens.
No amount of oversampling prevents eventual ESS collapse; the fundamental
scaling limit of the MCMC-free approach is set by the dimensionality of
the configuration space and the mismatch between the Gaussian proposal and
the Wigner-molecule geometry of $|\Psi|^2$.

Physical validation confirms that accuracy against DMC is not the only criterion.
The Wigner-molecule diagnostics---shell topology, bond-orientational order,
Lindemann ratios, and shell-mixture reconstruction of $g(r)$---establish that
the trained states are physically correct across the full Fermi-liquid-to-Wigner
crossover, not merely energetically competitive.

\paragraph{Layer 3\,---\,The optimization: matching the method to the regime.}
The third decision is how to minimize the energy given the chosen paradigm.
Two choices are not interchangeable: the gradient estimator and the optimizer.

The REINFORCE estimator is a structural requirement, not a numerical convenience.
Direct backpropagation through the Laplacian near nodal surfaces and
electron-electron coalescence produces pathological gradients; the training signal
is largest precisely where the gradient path is most unstable.
REINFORCE treats the local energy as a scalar reward, bypasses the Laplacian
in the gradient computation, and produces gradients that act only through the
score function $\nabla_\theta\log|\Psi_\theta|$, which is smooth near the
singularities even when the Laplacian is not.
Pure REINFORCE ($\beta = 0$) is strictly superior to hybrid objectives; the
direct weak-form term provides a cheaper energy proxy that interferes with
the variational signal rather than reinforcing it.

The optimizer must be matched to the regime.
Stochastic reconfiguration (SR) is the natural-gradient update on the variational
manifold; it accelerates convergence dramatically when the Fisher information
estimate is reliable---that is, when the ESS is high and the proposal achieves
good overlap with $|\Psi|^2$.
When ESS collapses, the Fisher estimate is dominated by a handful of high-weight
samples, and SR amplifies noise rather than curvature.
Below $\omega \lesssim 0.01$ or beyond $N \approx 12$, Adam's per-parameter
schedule is more robust.
This handoff is not a tuning parameter; it follows directly from the geometry
of the sampling problem.
The Langevin refinement experiment---applying overdamped Langevin dynamics to
move samples toward $|\Psi|^2$ before importance resampling---was a clean
negative result: a $+153\%$ energy error at $N{=}20$, $\omega{=}0.1$, caused by
non-equilibrium samples that invalidated the importance weights.
Any strategy that modifies samples before resampling must account for the changed
effective proposal, or the weights are simply wrong.

\paragraph{Open questions.}
Three questions remain concretely open.
First, the ESS scaling wall: the fundamental limit of the MCMC-free approach
is the exponential degradation of ESS with $Nd$.
Normalizing flows or diffusion-model proposals trained concurrently with the
wavefunction are natural candidates for breaking this limit.
Second, warm-started backflow at $N{=}20$: the evidence now clearly supports
a freeze-then-unfreeze strategy---training a memory-efficient backflow head
on top of the converged Jastrow checkpoint---and the gradient-quality argument
for why this ordering matters is now much cleaner than it was at the time of
the original decision.
Third, the Fisher matrix under partial overlap: the SR-to-Adam regime boundary
is currently determined empirically; a principled criterion for when the Fisher
estimate is too noisy to be useful would make the optimizer choice automatable
rather than regime-specific.

\paragraph{Broader implications.}
The three-layer decomposition is not specific to quantum dots.
Any neural-network variational calculation on a fermionic system in continuous
space must make the same three decisions: what to parameterize (Layer~1),
how to compute energies and train (Layer~2), and how to optimize (Layer~3).
The quantum dot setting made these decisions legible by providing DMC references,
known structural diagnostics, and a clean $(N,\omega)$ grid across which each
choice's consequences are visible.
The specific resolutions found here---physical constraints by construction,
MCMC-free training with REINFORCE, SR where it helps and Adam where it does
not---represent transferable principles rather than system-specific workarounds.
Whether the same framework extends to three-dimensional atoms, molecular crystals,
or the Hubbard model at finite doping is the natural next question.
The three layers suggest it will, and the engineering record here suggests what
each extension will require.

The variational principle guarantees one thing: the energy computed is an upper
bound.
One knows, at all times, when one is wrong.
The record assembled here---the Langevin failure, the SR-to-Adam handoff, the
ESS collapse, the function-space separation of correlator and backflow---is most
useful not as a list of solutions but as a map of where the upper bound stops
improving and why.
Any practitioner who builds the next version of this system will encounter the
same geometry.
The map is the contribution.
