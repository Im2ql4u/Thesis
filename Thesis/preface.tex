\vspace{2em}

When I came to UiO, Morten read my background and said: ``You know PINNs, you know
many-body quantum systems. I want you to do PINNs on quantum dots.'' I said
``sure.'' I knew enough about each of those things separately to imagine that putting
them together would mostly be a technical problem.

It was not.

The difficult part was rarely producing a result. The difficult part was knowing what
a result meant. When a calculation stopped improving, there were always several
plausible explanations: the wavefunction might be too restrictive, the optimiser
unsuitable, the sampling insufficient, the physical regime genuinely harder, or
something in the implementation simply wrong. Much of this thesis grew out of trying
to tell those possibilities apart.

For a long time they would not come apart. The results kept coming back bad, and
behind every careful explanation stood a simpler one that grew more convincing each time:
perhaps it simply was not possible. For a while I saw no real alternative to accepting
that. I kept going anyway, less out of hope than out of a wish to understand, at the very
least, why it did not work.

The hardest stretch came when I could not manage even that---when I knew that something
did not work and had no idea why.

I spent part of that time in Rome, which helped in ways that had little to do with
physics. Elida helped more than anything.

Some of the answers, when they finally came, were humbling. For months an error in the
importance-sampling code had produced exactly the failure I was prepared to blame on the
method itself: accuracy that deteriorated as the particle number grew and the trap
weakened. It was a small error in a log-density, accumulated over the particles and then
exponentiated. Finding it did not make the problem easy, but it changed what ``not
possible'' meant. A failure could have a cause, and the cause could be found. After that,
confidence had to be earned by tests rather than inherited from the code, and a failed
calculation became evidence rather than a verdict.

This also changed what I thought the neural network was for. I began with the comparatively simple question of whether a machine-learning ansatz could solve the many-body Schr\"odinger equation. By the end, the trained wavefunction had become something closer to an instrument. Its energy mattered, but so did the structure of its tangent space, the correlations carried by its internal representation, and the ways in which those changed across the physical regime. The network was no longer only a means of obtaining an answer; it had become an object through which the problem itself could be examined. That shift runs through the rest of this thesis.

It also made one connection hard to regard as accidental. Stochastic reconfiguration is
natural-gradient descent on the manifold of variational states; the REINFORCE estimator is
the score-function form of the variational energy gradient; for a real wavefunction, the
Fisher information matrix is, up to a factor of four, the quantum geometric tensor. The
physics and the optimisation had been describing the same terrain from opposite
directions.

The most consistent source of inspiration, motivation, and steadiness through all
of it has been my girlfriend, Elida. Her support made it possible to begin this
work and to stay with it when it became difficult. I am more grateful than I can
put down here.

I owe a warm and sincere thanks to my family, who have supported me in every
undertaking I have had and have simply always been there; and to my friends,
inside CCSE and out, who supplied the joy, the laughter, and a good deal of the
sanity this project required.

To Morten, again, and properly: I thank you for the assignment, for the two years we've had together,
and above all for a genuine and undisguised interest in what I was finding at
every stage---including the stages where what I was finding was that I had been
wrong. That interest is most of what encouragement actually consists of.

\bigskip

When I look back, it is easy to make a catalogue of what I would now do differently.
Most of that knowledge, however, was purchased by the failures and could not
have been read off in advance. In a sense, the regret and the result are the same object
seen from two sides.

I began this project thinking that the central difficulty would be finding a
sufficiently good answer to the many-body problem. More often, the difficult part was
discovering which question a disappointing result was actually answering.

I think that is the part of the work I will carry with me.
