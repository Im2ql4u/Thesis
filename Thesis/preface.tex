When I came to UiO, Morten read my background and said: ``You know PINNs, you know
many-body quantum systems. I want you to do PINNs on quantum dots.'' I said
``sure.'' I knew enough about each of those things separately to imagine that putting
them together would mostly be a technical problem.

It was not.

The difficult part was rarely producing a result. The difficult part was knowing what
a result meant. When a calculation stopped improving, there were always several
plausible explanations: the wavefunction might be too restrictive, the optimiser
unsuitable, the sampling insufficient, the physical regime genuinely harder, or
something in the implementation simply wrong. Much of this thesis grew out of trying
to tell those possibilities apart.

That is what variational methods make possible. The variational principle offers no recipe for improving a trial wavefunction, but it gives every attempt a direction: its energy cannot lie below the true ground-state energy, and a lower variational energy is therefore a better one. In a project with few fixed landmarks, that was enough to start. The harder question was what to make of a result that refused to improve.

There was no shortage of literature. Neural quantum states, FermiNet, PauliNet and neural backflow all provided examples to learn from. What was missing was a well-trodden path through the particular combination of methods I was trying to assemble. When an experiment failed, the failure rarely identified its cause. The method might be wrong, the architecture too restrictive, the optimiser ill-suited, the sampling poor, or the code simply broken. Often, several of these explanations looked equally plausible. Much of the work therefore became an exercise in designing calculations that could force them apart.

One failure made that need especially clear. An error in the importance-sampling code survived for months because it produced exactly the behaviour I had been prepared to attribute to the model itself: accuracy deteriorated as the particle number grew and the confinement weakened. The mechanism was simple in retrospect. A small error in a per-particle log-density accumulates over \(N\) particles and is then exponentiated in the importance weights. Where the calculations were cheap, the effect was barely visible; where the physics became interesting, it was catastrophic. Finding the bug did more than invalidate a set of runs. It made clear how easily a numerical failure could masquerade as a physical or architectural one. After that, confidence had to be earned by tests rather than inherited from the code.

The same lesson returned in other forms. A Langevin refinement meant to improve the proposal instead biased the gradient. A second-order optimiser became least trustworthy precisely when the metric it attempted to invert was no longer resolved by the available samples. These were useful results not because they were negative, but because each failure exposed an assumption that had previously gone untested. In that sense, failure became part of the method: not something to be hidden once a better calculation had been found, but evidence about where the calculation ceased to be reliable.

This also changed what I thought the neural network was for. I began with the comparatively simple question of whether a machine-learning ansatz could solve the many-body Schr\"odinger equation. By the end, the trained wavefunction had become something closer to an instrument. Its energy mattered, but so did the structure of its tangent space, the correlations carried by its internal representation, and the ways in which those changed across the physical regime. The network was no longer only a means of obtaining an answer; it had become an object through which the problem itself could be examined. That shift runs through the rest of this thesis.

It also made one connection increasingly difficult to regard as accidental. The obstacles encountered here are simultaneously problems in physics and in optimisation, and the same mathematical objects appear on both sides under different names. Stochastic reconfiguration, introduced in variational quantum Monte Carlo, is natural-gradient descent on the manifold of variational states. The REINFORCE estimator is the score-function form of the variational energy gradient. For a real wavefunction, the Fisher information matrix is, up to a conventional factor of four, the quantum geometric tensor. What initially looked like a collection of techniques borrowed from neighbouring fields gradually revealed itself as a common geometric language. Recognising that felt less like finding a bridge between two subjects than realising that they had been describing the same terrain from opposite directions.

\bigskip

When I look back, it is easy to make a catalogue of what I would now do differently.
Most of that knowledge, however, was purchased by the failures and could not
have been read off in advance. In a sense, the regret and the result are the same object
seen from two sides.

I began this project thinking that the central difficulty would be finding a
sufficiently good answer to the many-body problem. More often, the difficult part was
discovering which question a disappointing result was actually answering.

I think that is the part of the work I will carry with me.
