\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}

When I came to UiO, Morten read my background and said: ``You know PINNs, you know
many-body quantum systems. I want you to do PINNs on quantum dots.'' I said
``sure,'' and then asked the only sensible follow-up question I had:
``Where do I start?''

This thesis is the long answer. The short one took me the better part of two
years to find, and it is this: you start by arranging to be wrong in a way you
can measure.

That is what variational methods are for. The variational principle guarantees
that any trial wavefunction yields an energy above the true ground state, so
progress is unambiguous---the energy goes down or it does not---and the direction
of error is fixed by a theorem. What remains open is only how close you can get,
and at what cost. For a project with as few fixed landmarks as this one had, that
guarantee was often the only solid ground available.

I say ``two years'' deliberately. Morten gave me not the customary year but
nearly two, and the difference is the whole thesis: most of what is worth reading
here comes from the second year, and none of it would have been found on a
schedule that ended at the first.

It was, even so, a solitary way to spend them---not for want of a literature,
since neural quantum states are a busy field and FermiNet, PauliNet, and neural
backflow were all there to learn from, but because the particular thing I was
trying to do had very little in front of it to steer by. When an experiment
failed there was frequently no way to tell whether the method was unsound, the
architecture too weak, or the code quietly broken, and for long stretches the
honest answer was that I did not know. My friends at CCSE, and outside it, are the
reason those stretches remained survivable rather than merely long.

What kept me at it was a question more interesting than the one I was assigned.
Nearly every established method for the many-body Schr\"odinger equation is built
outward from physical principles: an approximation is chosen, justified
physically, and then implemented. I wanted to approach it from the other end---to
solve the problem on machine-learning terms, and only then ask what the solution
had understood. A trained network is not merely an answer; it is an answer with an
internal structure that can be examined. Twenty electrons in two dimensions is
forty degrees of freedom, and a configuration-interaction treatment of that would
outlast the age of the universe---but the wavefunction that solves it need not be
forty-dimensional in any meaningful sense. So: how many directions does the
trained state actually vary along, and how does that change as particles are added
or the trap is opened and they are given room to move apart?

Those questions have answers here, and they are more specific than I expected.
The most useful of them is that what separates one architecture from another is
not capacity but \emph{alignment} with the structure the problem actually has---a
distinction that costs nothing at strong confinement, where it is invisible, and
becomes the whole story when the trap is opened. Somewhere in the middle of that
analysis the network stopped being the object of study and became the apparatus,
which was not something I anticipated when I said ``sure.''

Yet the moments I learned the most from were, without exception, the failures. An
error in the importance-sampling code went undetected for months, because its
signature---accuracy decaying at larger particle numbers and weaker
confinement---is precisely the signature of an architecture that is merely not good
enough. In $Nd$ dimensions an error in a log-density grows linearly with dimension
and is amplified catastrophically in the normalised weights; the bug was invisible
exactly where experiments were cheap and ruinous exactly where they mattered.
Discovering it did real damage to my confidence in everything else, and
re-establishing that confidence rather than assuming it is a substantial part of
what the later chapters are doing. Two further failures---a Langevin refinement
step that biased the gradient, and a second-order optimiser that degraded
precisely when the landscape became ill-conditioned enough to require it---taught
the same lesson from different directions.

In each case the failure specified something no success could have: exactly what
the method had been quietly assuming, and exactly where the problem quietly
violated it. Negative results of this kind are not a concession or an
embarrassment. They are a method, and I have written them up as one.

Something else surfaced along the way that I have not stopped thinking about. The
obstacles in this work have a persistent double character: each is simultaneously
a physics problem and an optimisation problem, and it is usually arbitrary which
name you give it. Stochastic reconfiguration, invented by physicists to optimise
variational wavefunctions, is natural gradient descent on the variational
manifold. The REINFORCE estimator from reinforcement learning is the
score-function gradient of the variational energy. The Fisher information matrix
of statistics is the quantum geometric tensor of physics. These were not borrowed
by one field from another; they were found independently, by communities that
rarely read each other's papers. Coming to recognise that they are the same
mathematics felt less like discovering a bridge between two fields than like
realising there had only ever been one, approached from opposite sides.

What I can claim at the end is narrower than what I hoped for at the beginning,
and I have tried to keep it that way. Where an external benchmark exists, the
method matches it. Throughout the strongly correlated regime this thesis cares
most about, no such benchmark exists, and the energies there are variational
predictions supported by internal consistency and physical scaling rather than
validated numbers. I have said so in every place it is true. The temptation to
phrase the results as a competition with the best available methods was
considerable, and resisting it is one of the more defensible decisions in the
thesis.

Which returns me to hindsight. Looking back, my first instinct is to catalogue
what I would now do differently, and it is a long catalogue---very easy to
assemble once you already possess the knowledge you spent two years acquiring. But
most of it was purchased by the failures and could not have been read off in
advance from anything. The regret and the result are the same object seen from two
sides. That is worth remembering, and easy to forget, which is the best argument I
know for writing a preface at all.

\bigskip

The most consistent source of inspiration, motivation, and steadiness through all
of it has been my girlfriend, Elida. That support has been as constant as a
mountain, and without it I would not have had the nerve to begin this, let alone
the depth to finish it. I am more grateful than I can put down here.

I owe a warm and sincere thanks to my family, who have supported me in every
undertaking I have had and have simply always been there; and to my friends,
inside CCSE and out, who supplied the joy, the laughter, and a good deal of the
sanity this project required.

To Morten, again, and properly: thank you for the assignment, for the two years,
and above all for a genuine and undisguised interest in what I was finding at
every stage---including the stages where what I was finding was that I had been
wrong. That interest is most of what encouragement actually consists of.

\bigskip

Which is, at last, the answer to the question I asked on the first day. You start
by being willing to be wrong on the record, and then you keep the record.
