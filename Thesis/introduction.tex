\chapter*{Introduction}
In the classical world, we find much comfort with the idea of
reductionism: the belief that to understand the complicated whole, 
we merely need to dissect it into its constituent parts. 
Quantum mechanics rejects this entirely. 
At the fundamental scale, 
particles surrender their isolated individuality to a collective, 
deeply entangled web where the whole is not just greater than the sum of its parts, 
but entirely inseparable from them.
This is especially true in the quantum many-body-problem, 
goverened by the Schrödinger equation.
Given $N$ interacting particles in an external field, one writes down a
second-order partial differential equation on a configuration space of
dimension $Nd$ and asks for its ground state.
The difficulty is not the equation but the space: its size grows
exponentially with $N$, and ever techniques that works cleanly for two
particles often run aground long before twenty [<citation>] .
The competition between kinetic energy, which delocalizes particles across
this space, the potential energy localizing them, and interaction energy, which correlates them strongly, takes
place in a geometry too large to treat naively and too structured to ignore.

A wide range of methods has been developed to address this.
Mean-field approaches reduce the full many-body problem to an effective
single-particle picture, capturing Fermi statistics and exchange but
leaving most correlation physics untreated.
Full configuration interaction provide more
systematic hierarchy but scale combinatorially [<citation>].
Quantum Monte Carlo methods---Variational and Diffusion---sidestep
the basis explosion by representing the wavefunction implicitly through
stochastic sampling, and for suitable trial states they produce highly
accurate benchmark energies [<citation>].
In all of these, the central computational question reduces, sooner or
later, to the same problem: how do you parameterize a function on a
$Nd$-dimensional domain, impose antisymmetry and known short-range
physics, and optimize it reliably against a noisy energy signal?

\paragraph{Physics-informed neural networks as a natural candidate.}
Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) offer a direct answer to
this question.
They embed differential operators and physical constraints directly into a
training objective, training a neural network to satisfy the governing
equation at sampled points rather than on a grid.
For the Schrödinger equation, this means minimizing the squared residual
$\|(\hat H - E)\Psi_\theta\|^2$ over a set of sampled configurations, or
equivalently using the energy expectation $\langle\Psi_\theta|\hat H|\Psi_\theta\rangle$
as a loss.
Neural networks are, in principle, universal approximators of the needed
correlation functions; they scale to high dimensions by design; and
modern automatic differentiation makes the gradient of the loss with
respect to network parameters straightforward to compute.

The naive approach, however, fails at several distinct levels.
The Schrödinger equation is not a generic PDE, and neural wavefunctions
are not generic function approximators.
The specific structure of the problem---Coulomb singularities, fermionic
antisymmetry, long-range localization at low confinement, and the need
for stochastic sampling rather than grid quadrature---creates obstacles
that are invisible in the formalism and catastrophic in practice.
This thesis is a systematic study of those obstacles, and of the design
choices, algorithmic innovations, and explicit negative results that
reveal them.

\paragraph{Three decisions, in order.}
Building a PINN for the quantum many-body problem requires three decisions,
and they must be made in the right order because each one constrains the next.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Layer 1\,---\,The ansatz.}
Before any training begins, one must decide what to train: what functional form the
wavefunction will take, and which physical constraints will be enforced by architecture
versus learned from data.
The constraints are non-negotiable.
Fermionic antisymmetry requires a Slater determinant.
Permutation invariance of the correlator requires an equivariant architecture---a
Deep Sets particle branch~\cite{zaheer2018deepsets}---but particle-only pooling
cannot represent two-body correlations, so a pairwise branch is not optional.
The Kato cusp conditions require either an analytic cusp term or a network
with sufficient near-field sensitivity; the former is more reliable and cheaper.
Every constraint that is hard-wired into the architecture is one that the optimizer
need not rediscover, and one that cannot be violated by an unfortunate gradient step.
Beyond the hard constraints, the ansatz must be expressive enough to capture the
physics of interest while scaling reasonably with $N$. The Deep Sets architecuture 
has been shown to alone consist of limited expressivity, and required additional
correlation terms to capture correlations [<citation> Haas].
Backflow transformations improve the nodal geometry of the Slater reference but
cost $\mathcal{O}(N^2\times h_{\rm bf})$ in memory.
The choice of which representation to use determines what the optimizer can reach,
and representation analysis of the trained networks reveals that backflow and the
Jastrow correlator occupy qualitatively different function spaces: the correlator
lives on a low-dimensional manifold ($r_{\rm eff} \lesssim 3$), while the
backflow displacement field has intrinsic dimension an order of magnitude larger.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Layer 2\,---\,The training paradigm.}
Once the ansatz is fixed, one must decide how to compute energies and train.
Traditionally, variational Monte Carlo (VMC) has been the method of choice: 
one samples configurations from $|\Psi_\theta|^2$ using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) 
and estimates the energy and its gradient from those samples. 
Optionally one can use diffusion Monte Carlo (DMC) to refine the energy further, 
using the VMC state as a trial wavefunction. In this thesis, I develop another approach: 
a Physics-informed-Neaural-Network directions, 
using a collocation pipeline that draws samples from an analytic Gaussian mixture proposal 
and estimates gradients via the REINFORCE formulation, avoiding the Markov chain entirely
during training.
VMC with MCMC sampling from $|\Psi_\theta|^2$ is accurate but carries autocorrelation
overhead that grows with $N$ and correlation strength.
A fully collocation-trained Slater--Jastrow(+Backflow) network achieves $+0.009\%$
relative error against DMC at $(N{=}6,\,\omega{=}1.0)$ and sub-$0.25\%$ errors
across all five confinement strengths tested for $N{=}6$, without a single MCMC step.
The binding constraint on this approach is the effective sample size (ESS) of the
importance-weighted batch, which degrades with $Nd$ and small $\omega$ and defines
the scaling wall for the MCMC-free method.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Layer 3\,---\,The optimization.}
Given the ansatz and the training paradigm, one must choose how to minimize the energy.
This is not a generic optimization problem: the Schrödinger equation imposes structure
that distinguishes it sharply from standard deep learning.
Backpropagating through the Laplacian near nodal surfaces and electron-electron
coalescence produces pathological gradient signals---the training signal is largest
precisely where the gradient path is most unstable.
The resolution is the REINFORCE gradient estimator, which treats the local energy
as a scalar reward and computes gradients only through the score function
$\nabla_\theta\log|\Psi_\theta|$, bypassing the Laplacian entirely.
This is not a numerical convenience; it is a structural requirement for training
backflow-augmented wavefunctions in regimes where the nodal surface has fine structure.
The choice of optimizer compounds this: stochastic reconfiguration (SR), the natural
gradient on the variational manifold, accelerates convergence dramatically in
well-conditioned regimes but degrades catastrophically when the Fisher information
estimate is dominated by a handful of high-weight samples at low ESS.
Adam, with its per-parameter momentum schedule, is more robust precisely when SR fails.
The regime boundary between SR and Adam is physically interpretable, and the failure
modes on both sides are instructive: the Langevin refinement strategy, which attempted
to improve ESS by moving samples before importance resampling, produced a
$+153\%$ energy error because non-equilibrium samples invalidate the importance weights.

\paragraph{What this thesis records.}
This thesis documents the three decisions in the order they arise, and attends as
carefully to what failed as to what succeeded.
The gradient-estimator ablations, the Langevin negative result, the ESS collapse at
large $N$: these are not footnotes.
They are the primary record.
A design choice that has never been tested against its failure mode has not been understood,
and the map of where the upper bound stops improving is, in the end, more useful than any
single table of final energies.

\paragraph{Two-dimensional quantum dots as the testbed.}
These three decisions are studied concretely on two-dimensional parabolic quantum
dots: $N\in\{2,6,12,20\}$ electrons confined by a harmonic trap of frequency
$\omega$ and repelling via the Coulomb potential.
Quantum dots are an ideal testbed for three reasons.
First, they are genuinely nontrivial: Coulomb singularities, strong correlations,
and a real crossover from Fermi-liquid to Wigner-molecule physics appear already
at moderate $N$.
Second, accurate diffusion Monte Carlo (DMC) references exist across most of the
$(N,\omega)$ grid, providing a stringent external benchmark.
Third, the system is symmetric and compact enough that detailed structural
diagnostics (shell occupancies, bond-orientational order, virial ratios) and
representation analyses are computationally feasible, allowing us to verify
that the trained states capture not just the energy but the correct physics.

As $\omega$ decreases, the system passes through a Fermi-liquid--to--Wigner
crossover: electrons localize into ring or shell configurations that minimize
Coulomb repulsion while balancing the trap potential.
For $N{=}6$ and $N{=}12$, classical and semiclassical studies have mapped
preferred topologies: $(1,5)$ and $(0,6)$ for six electrons, $(3,9)$ for twelve
\cite{Egger_1999,Kong_2002,schweigert1994spectralpropertieschargedparticles,manninen2007metalclustersquantumdots}.
Quantitative topology-resolved diagnostics (shell fractions, bond order,
Lindemann ratios) from the variational neural ansatz provide an independent
route to the same physics and confirm that the trained states lie on the correct
side of the crossover.

\paragraph{Main contributions.}
The contributions of this thesis are organized around the three layers.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Ansatz design and physical constraints (Layer~1).}
        We specify a compact Slater--Jastrow PINN that satisfies antisymmetry,
        permutation equivariance, and the Kato cusp conditions by construction,
        with a pairwise branch that is necessary for correlated systems and an
        optional backflow component for improved nodal geometry.
        In trap-unit coordinates with soft-core pair features and an analytic
        cusp, this ansatz achieves DMC-level energies for $N{=}2$ and
        maintains $10^{-2}$--$10^{-1}$\,\% relative errors for
        $N\in\{6,12\}$ across three decades of confinement.
        Representation analysis reveals that the correlator occupies a
        low-dimensional manifold ($r_{\rm eff}\lesssim 3$), while the
        backflow displacement field has substantially higher intrinsic
        dimension---a separation that connects directly to the
        function-space geometry of the two architectures.

  \item \textbf{Comparative training paradigms and physical validation (Layer~2).}
        We compare neural VMC (MCMC-based) and MCMC-free collocation training
        against the hierarchy of established methods (HF, FCI, and DMC)
        across the $(N,\omega)$ grid.
        The MCMC-free pipeline achieves $+0.009\%$ relative error at
        $(N{=}6,\,\omega{=}1.0)$ and sub-$0.25\%$ for all five confinement
        strengths tested---without any Markov-chain sampling during training.
        Physical validation using Wigner-molecule diagnostics (shell topology,
        bond-orientational order, pair distribution reconstruction,
        cosine similarity $0.989$--$1.000$) confirms that the trained states
        are physically correct, not merely energetically competitive.

  \item \textbf{Optimization under Schrödinger constraints (Layer~3).}
        We establish that REINFORCE gradient decoupling is a structural
        requirement for training backflow-augmented wavefunctions, and that
        pure REINFORCE ($\beta{=}0$) is strictly superior to hybrid
        direct/REINFORCE objectives.
        We map the SR-to-Adam regime boundary as a function of $(N,\omega)$
        and provide a mechanistic explanation: SR degrades when ESS collapse
        makes the Fisher estimate rank-deficient.
        The Langevin refinement strategy is shown to be formally incorrect
        under importance resampling, with the $+153\%$ error at $N{=}20$
        establishing a clean constraint on future MCMC-free refinement strategies.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Thesis structure.}
The thesis is organized as follows.
The Theory chapter develops the quantum mechanical and machine-learning foundations
that each layer draws on: antisymmetry and the many-body problem,
the hierarchy of computational methods and the quantum dot model,
and optimization theory through natural gradients and PINNs.
The Methods chapters implement the three decisions in order: the ansatz
and then the training pipeline.
The Results chapter provides the comparative study---accuracy against HF,
FCI, and DMC; scaling with $N$; Wigner-molecule physics; and the
representation geometry of the trained networks.
The Discussion interprets all three layers against the results.
The appendix provides the formal conditioning analysis underlying 
and the complete experimental record of the optimization campaigns.
